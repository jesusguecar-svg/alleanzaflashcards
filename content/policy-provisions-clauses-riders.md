# General Lines Life, Accident, and Health Agent Exam Domain on Policy Provisions, Clauses, and Riders

## Domain snapshot

This domain tests how insurance contracts are **built, constrained, and interpreted** at the policy level: the standardized provisions that must appear in accident & health policies, the optional provisions insurers may add, and the riders and renewability rights that change what the policy does and does not do. The exam blueprint treats “policy riders, provisions, options, and exclusions” as a **high-weight domain (15%)**, and it explicitly expects recognition of common provisions like **entire contract, insuring clause, free look, consideration, grace period, reinstatement, incontestability, and misstatement of age and gender**. citeturn49view0turn49view1turn48view0

Why it’s heavily tested:

- These provisions are where **timing rules** live (deadlines for notices, proof of loss, suit limitations, etc.). Timing rules are among the easiest concepts to convert into multiple-choice questions with “one correct number.” citeturn20view0turn30view2turn32view0turn25view0turn35view1  
- These provisions determine **who has which rights** (policyowner/insured/beneficiary/insurer), which is core contract logic and a common exam focus. citeturn31view0turn34view0turn33view0  
- The standardized accident & health provisions in the entity["book","Texas Insurance Code","texas statutes compilation"] create predictable “default rules” (grace period minimums, reinstatement mechanics, proof-of-loss deadlines, etc.) that the exam can test precisely. citeturn30view0turn30view1turn32view0turn25view0  

Highest-yield subtopics (most testable):

- **Incontestable / time limit on certain defenses** (2-year rule + fraud exception + preexisting condition limitation after 2nd anniversary). citeturn29view0  
- **Grace period** (7/10/31 minimums) vs **reinstatement** (45-day rule, accident immediate, sickness after 10 days). citeturn30view0turn30view1  
- Claims “pipeline” deadlines: **notice of claim (21 days)** → **claim forms (by day 16)** → **proof of loss (by day 91)** → **time of payment** (immediate vs periodic at least monthly) → **legal actions** (not before day 61; not after 3 years). citeturn20view0turn30view2turn32view0turn25view0turn35view1  
- **Payment of claims** (who is paid; optional direct-to-provider payments; $1,000 max for certain “equitable” payments). citeturn33view0  
- **Change of occupation / illegal occupation / relation of earnings** (classic disability-income exam area). citeturn26view2turn27view0turn26view0  
- **Probationary (waiting) period vs elimination period** (and Texas’s maximum waiting period rules). citeturn52view0turn52view1  
- **Renewability rights** and the *labeling/disclosure requirements* for renewability on the first page. citeturn38view0turn40view1turn53view2turn37search0  

Most commonly confused on exam day:

- **Grace period vs reinstatement** (coverage continues vs coverage is “turned back on” after lapse). citeturn30view0turn30view1  
- **Notice of claim vs claim forms vs proof of loss** (three separate steps with three different clocks). citeturn20view0turn30view2turn32view0  
- **Time of payment of claims vs payment of claims** (when paid vs to whom/how paid). citeturn25view0turn33view0  
- **Probationary waiting period vs elimination period vs preexisting condition clause** (often swapped in distractors). citeturn52view0turn52view1turn52view2  
- **Guaranteed renewable vs noncancellable vs cancelable** (and what can change: policy vs premium). citeturn38view0turn53view2turn40view1  

## Deep explanation by subtopic

### Mandatory and optional provisions

Below, “Exam-tested facts” are the high-probability items to memorize; “Practical understanding” helps you reason through scenarios; “Memory aids” give fast recall; “Likely exam traps” are how distractors tend to be built.

**Entire contract**

Plain-English definition: The policy + attached documents are the whole deal; changes must be formally approved, and agents can’t “verbally change” coverage.

Why it exists: Prevents post-sale disputes and “side promises,” and locks down who can modify the contract.

How it works in health/disability: Endorsements/riders must be attached/endorsed and approved; the agent’s statements do not alter terms.

Protects insured against: Hidden terms outside the policy; unauthorized changes.

Rights given:
- Insurer: Only executive officer–approved changes are valid.
- Insured/owner: Contract is limited to what is written and attached; predictable enforcement.

Texas exam-tested facts: The required language states that changes are not valid unless approved by an executive officer and endorsed/attached; agents lack authority to change/waive provisions. citeturn31view0  

Most likely exam traps:
- Treating an agent’s promise as binding coverage.
- Confusing “entire contract” with “consideration.” (Different clauses; both can appear on exams.) citeturn31view0turn49view1  

Memory hook: **“Only what’s attached counts.”**

**Time limit on certain defenses (incontestable)**

Plain-English definition: After a set time, the insurer can’t use most application misstatements to void coverage or deny certain claims, except for fraud; and after that time, undisclosed preexisting conditions generally can’t be used to deny claims unless excluded by name.

Why it exists: Balances underwriting protection (early) with long-term certainty (later).

How it works in health/disability:
- After the **2nd anniversary**, non-fraud misstatements generally can’t void/deny claims for loss/disability that begins after that anniversary. citeturn29view0  
- Claims for loss/disability beginning after the 2nd anniversary can’t be reduced/denied based on a preexisting condition unless it was excluded **by name or specific description** effective on the date of loss. citeturn29view0  

Protects insured against: Late rescission for innocent errors; late “preexisting condition” denials (after the 2-year window) unless specifically excluded.

Rights given:
- Insurer: During the initial 2 years, can contest per law and policy; fraud is carved out.
- Insured: Stronger claim security after the contestability period.

Texas-specific nuance you should know:
- The statute contains a two-part “Time Limit on Certain Defenses” clause (misstatements + preexisting condition limitation). citeturn29view0  
- There’s an alternate incontestability clause for certain long-continuation policies that uses “two years during the lifetime of the insured (excluding any period of disability).” citeturn29view0  

Likely exam traps:
- “Incontestable means insurer can never deny a claim.” False.
- Missing that **fraud** is treated differently.
- Missing that it applies to **claims beginning after** the 2nd anniversary (timing is a classic distractor). citeturn29view0  

Memory hook: **“2 years → truth locks in (except fraud), and unnamed preexist loses power.”**

**Grace period**

Plain-English definition: A short window after a premium due date in which coverage stays active even if the premium hasn’t been paid yet.

Why it exists: Prevents accidental lapse due to minor payment delays.

How it works in health/disability: Coverage continues during the grace period; if a loss happens during grace, you don’t automatically lose coverage (though the policy may handle unpaid premium via deduction in some contexts).

Texas exam-tested minimums (individual A&H):
- At least **7 days** (weekly premium),
- At least **10 days** (monthly premium),
- At least **31 days** (all other policies). citeturn30view0  

Texas-specific disclosure wrinkles:
- Policies with cancellation provisions may add language acknowledging the insurer’s cancellation right. citeturn30view0  
- If the insurer reserves the right to refuse renewal, the grace provision must incorporate (or be preceded by) a notice concept tied to nonrenewal timing (test writers sometimes use this to blur renewability vs grace). citeturn30view0  

Most likely exam traps:
- Treating grace period as “policy reinstatement.”
- Picking the wrong day-count for the premium mode. citeturn30view0  

Memory hook: **“Grace keeps you covered; reinstatement brings you back.”**

**Reinstatement**

Plain-English definition: Restoring coverage after a lapse from nonpayment, usually by paying overdue premium and possibly submitting evidence of insurability.

Why it exists: Gives insured a path back without a brand-new policy (but with protections for the insurer).

How it works in health/disability (Texas individual A&H):
- If a premium isn’t paid by the end of the grace period, later acceptance of premium can reinstate. citeturn30view1  
- If the insurer requires an application and issues a conditional receipt, the policy is reinstated upon approval, or automatically on the **45th day** after the conditional receipt date unless disapproved earlier in writing. citeturn30view1  
- After reinstatement: **accident** losses are covered if the injury occurs after reinstatement; **sickness** is covered only if it begins **more than 10 days** after reinstatement. citeturn30view1  
- Premium accepted for reinstatement can’t be applied to periods more than **60 days** before reinstatement. citeturn30view1  

Protects insured against: Permanent loss of coverage from temporary nonpayment.

Protects insurer against: People waiting to pay until after they get sick (10-day sickness delay).

Likely exam traps:
- Saying sickness coverage is immediate upon reinstatement (it’s not, per the required provision). citeturn30view1  
- Confusing “45-day reinstatement” with the “31-day” grace period. citeturn30view0turn30view1  

Memory hook: **“Reinstate: 45 to accept; Accident now, Sickness +10.”**

**Notice of claim**

Plain-English definition: The first formal heads-up to the insurer that a covered loss occurred.

Why it exists: Starts the claim process while facts are fresh.

How it works (Texas individual A&H):
- Written notice must be given **by the 21st day** after the occurrence or beginning of loss, or as soon as reasonably possible. citeturn20view0  
- For long-term loss-of-time (disability) benefits (payable ≥2 years), the policy can require periodic notices of continued disability at intervals of **1–6 months**, with rules preventing unfair forfeiture from delay. citeturn20view0  

Protects insured against: Claim denial solely from minor delay when “reasonably possible” standard is met.

Rights given:
- Insurer: Early notice to investigate.
- Insured: Ability to satisfy notice via authorized agent with enough info to identify insured. citeturn20view0  

Likely exam traps:
- Mixing this up with **proof of loss**.
- Missing the “as soon as reasonably possible” safety valve. citeturn20view0turn32view0  

Memory hook: **“Notice is early; proof is detailed.”**

**Claim forms**

Plain-English definition: The insurer’s standardized paperwork for submitting proof.

Why it exists: Standardizes what information is collected for underwriting and payment.

How it works (Texas individual A&H):
- After receiving notice of claim, insurer provides claim forms.
- If forms aren’t provided **before the 16th day after notice**, the claimant can satisfy proof-of-loss requirements by submitting written proof covering the occurrence/character/extent within the proof-of-loss timeframe. citeturn30view2  

Likely exam traps:
- Choosing “15 days” instead of “16th day” (Texas wording is framed as “before the 16th day”). citeturn30view2  

Memory hook: **“No forms by day 16 → you can file your own written proof.”**

**Proof of loss**

Plain-English definition: The detailed written documentation that substantiates the claim (medical records, bills, physician statements, disability statements, etc., depending on coverage).

Why it exists: Prevents paying unsupported claims; defines the insurer’s obligation to pay.

How it works (Texas individual A&H):
- Periodic-payment claims: proof due **before the 91st day after the period ends** for which insurer is liable. citeturn32view0  
- Other losses: proof due **before the 91st day after the date of loss**. citeturn32view0  
- Late proof does not invalidate/reduce claim if not reasonably possible; must be provided ASAP but **no later than 1 year** after the time it would otherwise be required, except legal incapacity. citeturn32view0  

Likely exam traps:
- Replacing 91 days with 90 days.
- Forgetting the “1-year outer limit” exception structure. citeturn32view0  

Memory hook: **“Proof by day 91; hard stop at 1 year (unless incapacity).”**

**Time of payment of claims**

Plain-English definition: When the insurer must pay once it has due proof.

Why it exists: Prevents indefinite delay after proof is received.

How it works (Texas individual A&H):
- Non-periodic losses: paid **immediately** upon receipt of due written proof. citeturn25view0  
- Periodic (e.g., disability income): accrued indemnities paid at a stated interval, but **not less frequent than monthly**, and remaining balance paid immediately upon termination of liability with proof. citeturn25view0  

Likely exam traps:
- Confusing this with **Payment of claims** (who gets paid).
- Confusing this with separate statutory “prompt pay” regimes (a favorite distractor). citeturn25view0turn33view0turn18view0  

Memory hook: **“Time = when; Payment = who.”**

**Payment of claims**

Plain-English definition: Who receives claim payments and optional methods (e.g., paying a hospital directly).

Why it exists: Prevents disputes over payee; supports provider-direct payment where allowed.

How it works (Texas individual A&H):
- Death indemnity payable per beneficiary designation or, if none effective, to the insured’s estate. citeturn33view0  
- Other accrued indemnities unpaid at death: may be paid to beneficiary designation or estate (insurer option). citeturn33view0  
- Other indemnities payable to the insured. citeturn33view0  
- Optional provisions: insurer may pay up to a specified amount to certain relatives if payee is estate/minor/incompetent; cap may not exceed **$1,000**. citeturn33view0  
- Optional direct-to-provider payments for hospital/medical/surgical services unless insured requests otherwise in writing before or at proof submission. citeturn33view0  

Likely exam traps:
- Saying insurer must always pay providers directly (it’s optional and subject to insured direction). citeturn33view0  
- Forgetting the $1,000 cap. citeturn33view0  

Memory hook: **“Life to beneficiary; living benefits to insured; provider-pay is optional.”**

**Physical examination and autopsy**

Plain-English definition: Insurer can require physical exams while claim is pending and, if death, can require an autopsy (where lawful), all at insurer expense.

Why it exists: Anti-fraud and claim verification.

How it works:
- Exam “when and as often as reasonably required” during a pending claim.
- Autopsy allowed if not forbidden by law. citeturn35view0  

Likely exam traps:
- Saying insured must pay for exam/autopsy (insurer pays). citeturn35view0  

Memory hook: **“Insurer verifies at insurer’s expense.”**

**Legal actions**

Plain-English definition: A contractual suit limitation clause: when you can sue and how long you have.

Why it exists: Gives claim process time to work; prevents stale claims.

How it works (Texas individual A&H):
- No lawsuit before the **61st day** after written proof of loss is provided as required. citeturn35view1  
- No lawsuit after **3 years** after proof of loss is required to be provided. citeturn35view1  

Likely exam traps:
- Using 60 days instead of 61.
- Confusing the 3-year contractual limitation with other limitation periods. citeturn35view1  

Memory hook: **“Sue: not before 61; not after 3.”**

**Change of beneficiary**

Plain-English definition: Unless beneficiary is irrevocable, insured can change who receives benefits.

Why it exists: Keeps ownership control with the insured/owner and reflects life changes.

How it works (Texas individual A&H required provision):
- If no irrevocable designation, insured reserves right to change beneficiary and doesn’t need beneficiary consent for surrender/assignment/change/other changes. citeturn34view0  

Likely exam traps:
- Claiming beneficiary must consent to most changes (false for revocable beneficiaries). citeturn34view0  

Memory hook: **“Revocable = changeable.”**

**Misstatement of age or gender**

Plain-English definition: If key demographic info used for pricing is wrong, benefits are adjusted to what the paid premium would have purchased if correct.

Why it exists: Protects actuarial fairness without requiring full rescission.

How it works in health/disability policies (Texas A&H optional provision):
- If age misstated, amounts payable are what the premium would have bought at correct age. citeturn26view1  

How it works in life exam context:
- The exam blueprint explicitly flags “misstatement of age and gender” as a tested policy provision area. citeturn49view1turn48view0  
- A parallel concept exists in life policy regulation (“misstatement of age” rule language requiring benefit adjustment to what premium would have purchased at correct age). citeturn50search3  

Practical understanding: Gender/sex historically affected life pricing in many systems; modern market rules vary by product line and law, so the exam tends to test the **mechanism** (benefit adjustment) rather than contemporary rating debates. citeturn49view1turn50search3  

Likely exam traps:
- Treating misstatement as “automatic voiding.” (Usually not; it’s adjustment unless tied to fraud/incontestability timing.)
- Confusing this clause with incontestability (different purpose). citeturn29view0turn26view1turn49view1  

Memory hook: **“Wrong age → right benefit.”**

**Change of occupation**

Plain-English definition: If insured changes to a more or less hazardous occupation, the insurer adjusts benefits/premiums as specified.

Why it exists: Disability and accident risk varies by occupation.

How it works (Texas individual A&H optional provision):
- If insured changes to a more hazardous occupation (or does hazardous work for compensation), insurer pays only the portion of indemnity that the premium would have bought for that more hazardous occupation. citeturn26view2  
- If insured changes to a less hazardous occupation, insurer reduces premium and returns excess pro rata unearned premium back to the most recent of (a) occupational change date or (b) policy anniversary before proof received. citeturn26view2  
- Must use insurer’s occupational classifications and rates as last filed/made effective in the state of insured’s residence at issue. citeturn26view2  

Likely exam traps:
- Mixing up *more hazardous* vs *less hazardous* outcomes.
- Forgetting that hazardous “side work for compensation” triggers the more-hazardous treatment. citeturn26view2  

Memory hook: **“Risk up → benefit down; risk down → premium down + refund.”**

**Illegal occupation**

Plain-English definition: No coverage when the loss is caused in part by felony attempt/commission or by engaging in an illegal occupation.

Why it exists: Moral hazard control; avoids insuring illegal activity.

How it works (Texas individual A&H optional provision):
- Insurer not liable for loss where a contributing cause was felony commission/attempt or being engaged in an illegal occupation. citeturn27view0  

Likely exam traps:
- Thinking it requires sole cause. The text uses **“contributing cause.”** citeturn27view0  

Memory hook: **“Illegal + contributes = no benefits.”**

**Relation of earnings to insurance**

Plain-English definition: Disability “loss of time” benefits can’t exceed earnings; if total benefits exceed earnings, benefits are reduced proportionally and excess premium is returned.

Why it exists: Prevents overinsurance (getting paid more to be disabled than to work).

How it works (Texas optional provision, disability-focused):
- If total monthly loss-of-time benefits across all valid coverage exceed earnings (greater of (a) earnings at disability start or (b) average earnings for 2 years before disability), insurer pays only proportionate benefits and returns part of premiums paid in prior 2 years that exceed pro rata premium for benefits actually paid. citeturn26view0  
- It can’t reduce total monthly benefits under all coverage below **$200** or the sum of monthly benefits specified, whichever is less; and it doesn’t reduce non–loss-of-time benefits. citeturn26view0  
- This provision is only permitted in certain long-continuation policies (continuable until at least age 50, or at least 5th anniversary if issued after age 44). citeturn26view0  

Likely exam traps:
- Forgetting the **$200 floor**.
- Missing that it applies to **loss of time benefits** (disability income), not all benefits. citeturn26view0  

Memory hook: **“DI can’t beat your paycheck.”**

### Other provisions and clauses

**Insuring clause**

Definition: The clause that states what the insurer promises to do—what losses are covered and under what conditions.

Why it exists: It’s the core “coverage grant.”

How it works: Establishes the insurer’s basic obligation before exclusions/limitations carve it back.

Exam-tested anchor: The exam outline explicitly calls out “insuring clause” as a tested policy provision. citeturn49view1turn48view0  

Trap: Confusing insuring clause (what is covered) with exclusions (what is carved out).

Memory hook: **“Insuring clause = the ‘YES’ statement.”**

**Free look**

Definition: A right to return the policy within a specified period and receive a refund.

Why it exists: Consumer protection for “buyer’s remorse,” especially for complex health products and Medicare-related coverage.

Texas A&H angle (individual accident & health): Texas requires a “right to examine” type provision allowing the policyholder to void the policy by returning it within **10 days**, with refund of premiums paid. citeturn8search3  

Federal Medicare Supplement angle: Medigap has a **30-day free look** right (return policy within 30 days for a refund if you decide you don’t want it). citeturn7search1  

Trap: Defaulting to “30 days” for all health policies (30 is Medigap; many other policies use different periods). citeturn8search3turn7search1  

Memory hook: **“10-day Texas right-to-examine; 30-day Medigap free look.”**

**Consideration clause**

Definition: What each party gives in exchange: the insured pays premium (and makes application statements), and the insurer provides coverage.

Why it exists: Contract formation element and evidence of enforceability.

Exam-tested anchor: “Consideration” appears as a tested policy provision in the exam outline. citeturn49view1turn48view0  

Trap: Confusing consideration with entire contract (what forms the contract vs what is exchanged).

Memory hook: **“Premium = consideration.”**

**Probationary period**

Definition: A waiting period starting when a person is initially insured until certain coverages become effective.

Why it exists: Anti-adverse-selection (prevents someone from buying coverage after getting sick and immediately claiming).

How it works (Texas rule standard):
- Waiting period **cannot apply to accidental injury losses**. citeturn52view0  
- Typical maximum for sickness waiting period is **30 days**, but Texas allows longer waiting periods for certain categories:  
  - Pregnancy/childbirth/miscarriage/complications—when expressed in terms of inception of pregnancy, no more than 30 days; citeturn52view0  
  - Six months for certain listed conditions (hernia, reproductive organ disorders, varicose veins, hemorrhoids, appendix, tonsils, adenoids, gall bladder); citeturn52view0  
  - 120 days for specified disease insurance coverages. citeturn52view0  
- Waiting periods do **not** change preexisting condition limitations. citeturn52view0  

Exam trap: Calling an elimination period a waiting period, or applying waiting periods to accidents. citeturn52view0turn52view1  

Memory hook: **“Probation starts at issue; never blocks accidents.”**

**Elimination period**

Definition: The initial time during a covered disability/condition when benefits are not yet payable.

Why it exists: Cost control and alignment with short-term resources (sick leave, short-term disability, savings).

How it works (Texas framing): It relates to the initial period during continuance of the insured condition for which benefits won’t be paid and must be clearly shown or referenced in the schedule and benefit provisions. citeturn52view1  

Key difference from probationary: Elimination period is triggered by the **loss/disability event**, not by **policy effective date**. citeturn52view0turn52view1  

Memory hook: **“Elimination eliminates early days of a disability claim.”**

**Waiver of premium**

Definition: A feature/rider that suspends premium payments during a qualifying disability (or while benefits are being paid), so coverage stays in force without premium.

Why it exists: Prevents lapse when the insured can’t work and needs coverage most.

How it works:
- NAIC consumer guidance describes waiver of premium for disability insurance as exempting the insured from paying premiums while disabled (typically included in a policy). citeturn51search2  
- In long-term care context, NAIC describes waiver of premium as an insured receiving benefits no longer having to pay premiums. citeturn51search12  

Exam trap: Confusing “waiver of premium” with “future increase option” (one removes payments; the other increases coverage). citeturn51search2turn51search0  

Memory hook: **“Disabled → premiums waived, policy stays alive.”**

**Exclusions and limitations**

Definition:
- Exclusion/exception: a risk not assumed; coverage for a specified hazard is eliminated.
- Limitation: coverage exists but is restricted (amount, duration, scope).

Texas rule emphasis:
- A “reduction” is a clause that reduces part (not all) of coverage for a risk assumed, limiting payment amount or period. citeturn53view0  
- Exceptions/exclusions/reductions must be clearly expressed in the benefit provision or set forth separately and appropriately captioned. citeturn53view0  

Exam trap: Using “exclusion” when the policy only limits the benefit (e.g., max visits). citeturn53view0turn45search7  

Memory hook: **“Exclusion = no; limitation = less.”**

**Preexisting conditions**

Definition (Texas rule floor): A policy’s definition of “preexisting condition” can’t be more restrictive than symptoms that would cause a prudent person to seek diagnosis/care within a **5-year** period preceding effective date, or conditions for which medical advice/treatment was recommended/received within a **5-year** period preceding effective date. citeturn52view2  

Key A&H incontestability linkage: For claims beginning after the 2nd anniversary, the insurer can’t reduce/deny based on an undisclosed preexisting condition unless excluded by name or specific description effective on date of loss. citeturn29view0  

Texas “creditable coverage” mechanics (likely background more than direct exam-testing unless the exam goes deep into HIPAA-era rules):
- The rule describes when preexisting condition provisions may not apply (e.g., 18 months of continuous creditable coverage up to ≤63-day break in some circumstances), and requires compliance with notice/disclosure rules for creditable coverage. citeturn52view2  

Exam traps:
- Confusing a probationary waiting period with a preexisting condition clause (different triggers and different logic). citeturn52view0turn52view2  
- Thinking ACA bans all preexisting condition clauses in every health product (the exam often focuses on *contract provisions*; regulation varies by product type). citeturn52view2  

Memory hook: **“Preexisting = before effective date; probationary = after effective date but before coverage kicks in.”**

**Coinsurance**

Plain-English: A percentage share you pay after deductible, typically based on the plan’s allowed amount.

Key “allowed amount” anchor: The allowed amount is the maximum a plan will pay for a covered service and may be called “eligible expense,” “payment allowance,” or “negotiated rate.” citeturn45search1  

Exam trap: Calculating coinsurance on the provider’s billed charge instead of allowed amount.

Memory hook: **“Coinsurance is a percent of allowed amount.”**

**Deductibles**

Plain-English: The amount you pay before the plan begins paying (subject to coverage rules and services).

Exam trap: Confusing deductible (threshold) with copayment (flat fee) and coinsurance (percentage share).

**Eligible expenses**

Plain-English: Expenses recognized as covered/allowable under the plan.

Exam-tested anchor: “Allowed amount” may also be called “eligible expense,” linking the concept directly to how claims are calculated and cost-sharing is computed. citeturn45search1  

**Copayments**

Plain-English: Fixed-dollar amounts paid for certain services (e.g., $30 primary care visit), usually not expressed as a percentage.

Exam trap: Treating copay as a percentage (that’s coinsurance).

**Pre-authorizations and prior approval requirements**

Plain-English: Plan approval required before certain services for them to be covered.

Official definition anchor: “Preauthorization” is a plan decision that a service/treatment/drug/DME is medically necessary; it may be called prior authorization/approval/precertification; and it is **not a promise** of payment. citeturn45search2  

Exam trap: “If you got preauthorization, it must be covered.” Not necessarily. citeturn45search2  

Memory hook: **“Preauth = permission to try, not a payment guarantee.”**

**Usual, reasonable, and customary charges**

Plain-English: A benchmark amount based on what providers typically charge in a geographic area.

Official anchor: HealthCare.gov defines UCR as the amount paid for a service based on what providers in the area usually charge; it can be used to determine the allowed amount. citeturn45search0  

Exam trap: Confusing UCR with “Medicare-approved amount” (not the same concept).

**Lifetime, annual, or per cause maximum benefit limits**

Definitions:
- Lifetime limit: a cap on total lifetime benefits payable (can be total-dollar or service-specific caps). citeturn45search3  
- Annual limit: a cap on benefits payable in a year (dollar or visit-based). citeturn45search7  

Federal consumer-protection angle (ACA/EHB context): The health care law prevents insurers from imposing yearly or lifetime limits on **essential health benefits** (with exceptions for non-EHB benefits and certain plan categories). citeturn45search9turn45search12  

Texas consumer glossary reinforcement: TDI explains lifetime maximum as the total dollar amount the plan will pay over a lifetime and notes ACA prohibits most lifetime dollar limits on essential health benefits. citeturn45search21  

Exam traps:
- Assuming “lifetime/annual limits are always illegal.” They are restricted in many contexts, but exam questions often hinge on **plan type** and whether the limit is on EHB or not. citeturn45search9turn45search12  
- Confusing a “per cause” limit with an annual limit (per cause = per illness/injury episode category).

### Riders

**Impairment/exclusions rider**

Definition: A rider (endorsement) that excludes or restricts coverage for a specific impairment, body part, condition, or hazardous activity.

Why it exists: Enables underwriting—insurer can offer coverage while carving out known high-risk areas instead of declining entirely.

How it works (health/disability):
- Typically listed as a specific exclusion (e.g., no benefits for the back, or no claims arising from a known knee condition).
- Must be read together with the insuring clause and exclusions/limitations layout.

Exam-tested anchor: Texas rules emphasize that exclusions/reductions must be clearly expressed and appropriately captioned, which is how impairment riders are typically presented in policy form. citeturn53view0  

Likely exam traps:
- Treating an impairment rider as a “preexisting condition clause.” (A rider can be narrower and specifically named; a preexisting condition clause is a defined limitation category.) citeturn29view0turn52view2  

Memory hook: **“Rider = custom carve-out.”**

**Guaranteed insurability rider**

Definition: A rider that lets the insured increase coverage later without proving insurability again (no new medical exam), at specified times/conditions.

Why it exists: Protects insurability against future health changes; supports life events planning.

Official anchor: NAIC explains a guaranteed insurability rider allows increasing death benefit at certain future times without a medical exam, with cost dependent on age/amount, not health/lifestyle. citeturn51search0  

Exam trap: Confusing this with waiver of premium (one increases coverage; the other suspends premium). citeturn51search0turn51search2  

Memory hook: **“Guaranteed insurability = buy more later, no new underwriting.”**

**Future increase option**

Definition: A disability-focused option/rider allowing the insured to increase monthly benefit later (often tied to income increases or specified option dates) with limited or no medical underwriting.

Why it exists: Disability income needs rise with earnings; this keeps benefits aligned with lifestyle without rewriting the whole policy.

Authoritative support (closest conceptual anchor available): NAIC notes disability policies may offer ways to purchase additional coverage to keep up with raises/promotions since benefit payments do not automatically rise with income. citeturn51search2turn51search10  

Likely exam traps:
- Mistaking future increase option for COLA. (Future increase = buy more base benefit; COLA = increases benefits over time to offset inflation.)
- Thinking an increase option waives premiums. citeturn51search2turn51search10  

Memory hook: **“Future increase = more coverage; COLA = bigger checks later.”**

### Rights of renewability

**Noncancelable**

Plain-English definition: The insurer can’t cancel the policy and can’t raise the premium for that policy as long as premiums are paid on time (classically “locked” contract).

Texas form-labeling anchor: The renewability provision must match the type of contract and be properly captioned; “noncancellable” has a defined meaning under Texas rules. citeturn53view2  

Exam trap: Saying noncancelable means “can’t be canceled by either party.” The insured can usually stop paying or cancel; the restriction is on the insurer.

Memory hook: **“Noncancelable = insurer can’t cancel or re-rate you.”**

**Cancelable**

Plain-English definition: The insurer can cancel or nonrenew (subject to policy terms and applicable law) and can generally change premium/terms according to the contract.

Texas exam angle: Cancelable is typically tested as the “least protective” renewability classification (most insurer discretion).

Trap: Confusing cancelable with guaranteed renewable.

**Guaranteed renewable**

Plain-English definition: The insurer can’t cancel the policy as long as premiums are paid, but it can raise premiums on a **class basis** (not based on one person’s individual claim experience), consistent with policy terms and law.

Texas statutory backbone for individual hospital/medical/surgical coverage:
- Insurers must generally renew/continue at the individual’s option, with limited exceptions (nonpayment, fraud/misrepresentation, insurer ceasing to offer coverage in the market, service-area relocation under conditions, and as permitted by federal law/regulations). citeturn38view0turn40view1  
- Texas rule text states that, except as provided, individual hospital/medical/surgical coverage must be renewed or continued at the option of the insured. citeturn40view1  

Disclosure mechanics:
- If a policy reserves the right to increase premium at renewal or to nonrenew/terminate at a certain age, Texas rules require a 10-point type notice at the top of the first page, and also require renewability provisions be appropriately captioned and on/referenced from the first page. citeturn53view2turn37search0  

Exam trap: Confusing “guaranteed renewable” with “noncancellable.” Guaranteed renewable can still be repriced for the class. citeturn53view2turn38view0  

Memory hook: **“Guaranteed renewable = can’t cancel you, can re-rate the class.”**

## Exam-focused takeaways

### What to know cold

Mandatory A&H provision numbers and triggers:

- **Grace period minimums:** 7 / 10 / 31 days by premium mode. citeturn30view0  
- **Reinstatement:** 45-day automatic reinstatement if conditional receipt + no written disapproval; accident coverage immediate; sickness begins >10 days after reinstatement; premium not applied beyond 60 days back. citeturn30view1
