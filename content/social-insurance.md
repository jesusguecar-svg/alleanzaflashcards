# Texas General Lines Life, Accident, and Health Agent Exam — Social Insurance Domain Deep Research Memo

## Domain snapshot

This domain tests your working knowledge of major public/social insurance programs that frequently coordinate with (or displace) private life and health insurance benefits: **Medicare**, **Medicaid**, and (for this memo’s scope) **workers’ compensation**. The official test outline for Texas Life & Health places **Social Insurance at ~6%** of the exam and explicitly lists Medicare (Parts A–D) and Medicaid under this domain. citeturn23view0

Why it matters for the exam: the programs themselves are straightforward, but **test questions usually target the boundaries**—what each program covers vs. does not cover, who pays first, and which program is “needs-based” vs. “age/disability-based.” Those boundary questions are also where agents make costly mistakes in real sales conversations (especially with seniors and dual-eligible clients). citeturn29view1turn33view0turn32view1

Highest-yield subtopics (most testable):
- **Medicare Parts A/B/C/D**: what each part covers at a high level and how Part C “bundles” A and B (and usually D). citeturn29view1turn31view0turn31view1turn21search13turn11view1  
- **Eligibility and enrollment timing basics**: Initial Enrollment Period, General Enrollment Period, Special Enrollment Periods, and late-enrollment penalties (especially Part B and Part D). citeturn30view0turn11view0turn11view1turn30view1  
- **What Medicare doesn’t cover well** (or at all): long-term care/custodial care, most dental, routine vision/eye exams for glasses, hearing aids, routine physicals—commonly tested. citeturn29view0turn21search17  
- **Medicare vs. Medicaid** distinctions, including “Medicare pays first / Medicaid pays last” in dual coverage. citeturn29view1turn33view0  
- **Workers’ comp fundamentals and Texas’s unique opt-in structure** for most private employers, plus the “subscriber vs. non-subscriber” consequences. citeturn25view1turn16view0turn16view3  

Most likely confusion points on exam day:
- **Part A vs. Part B** (hospital-related vs. outpatient/medical-related). citeturn31view0turn31view1  
- **Original Medicare vs. Medicare Advantage (Part C)** (government-run fee-for-service vs. private plan alternative). citeturn29view1turn21search17turn11view1  
- **Part D vs. “drug coverage included in Medicare Advantage”** (Part D can be stand-alone with Original Medicare, or integrated in many MA plans). citeturn29view1turn31view2turn11view1  
- **Medigap vs. Medicaid** (private supplement vs. needs-based public program) and the rule that Medigap generally requires Original Medicare (not Medicare Advantage). citeturn29view1turn31view3turn4search1turn33view0  
- **Workers’ comp vs. disability income insurance** (work-related injury program vs. income-replacement insurance that can apply broadly depending on policy). citeturn25view0turn16view1turn26view0  

## Deep explanation by subtopic

### entity["organization","Medicare","federal health insurance program"]

1) **Plain-English definition (exam-tested fact)**  
Medicare is **federal health insurance**, primarily for people **65+**, and also available earlier for certain people with disabilities or conditions (for example, disability after a waiting period, ALS, ESRD under special rules). citeturn12search5turn30view1turn12search7turn30view0

2) **Problem the program is designed to solve (practical understanding that supports exam answers)**  
Medicare provides a baseline national system to help older adults and certain disabled individuals access and afford hospital and medical care, with defined benefits and cost-sharing. citeturn29view1turn31view0turn31view1  

3) **Who it covers (exam-tested fact)**  
Medicare covers:
- Most people at **65** (automatic or active enrollment depending on Social Security status). citeturn30view1turn30view0  
- Some people under 65 who receive disability benefits for a defined period, and people with **ALS** (earlier automatic Medicare), and eligibility pathways for **ESRD**. citeturn30view1turn12search0turn12search7  

4) **How eligibility generally works (exam-tested fact)**  
At a high level, eligibility is tied to age (65) or qualifying disability/condition pathways, and enrollment is coordinated through Social Security processes for many applicants (automatic enrollment in some cases). citeturn30view1turn12search19turn30view0  

5) **How benefits generally work (exam-tested fact)**  
Medicare benefits are divided into Parts:
- **Part A** for inpatient/hospital-related coverage;  
- **Part B** for outpatient/medical-related coverage;  
- **Part C (Medicare Advantage)** as a private-plan alternative that bundles at least A and B (and usually D);  
- **Part D** for prescription drugs through private plans following Medicare rules. citeturn29view1turn31view0turn31view1turn31view2turn11view1  

6) **Funding structure at a high level (testable at “headline” level)**  
Medicare is financed through **two trust funds**:  
- The **Hospital Insurance (HI) Trust Fund** (funding Part A) is funded largely by payroll taxes plus specific other sources.  
- The **Supplementary Medical Insurance (SMI) Trust Fund** (funding Parts B and D) is funded by congressional appropriations and premiums paid by enrollees. citeturn14view0  

7) **Coordination/interaction with private insurance (highly testable)**  
Key coordination concepts:
- Medicare can be **primary or secondary**, depending on other coverage (employer group health, retiree coverage, Medicaid, etc.); this is “coordination of benefits.” citeturn32view1turn32view0  
- Medicare “late enrollment penalties” exist if you delay enrollment without qualifying coverage or a Special Enrollment Period. citeturn11view0turn30view0  
- Medicare’s structure creates private-market companions: **Medigap (Medicare Supplement)** for Original Medicare cost-sharing, and Medicare Advantage (Part C) as a private alternative. citeturn29view1turn31view3turn4search1turn11view1  

8) **Key federal and Texas-specific angles (exam-tested vs. background)**  
- Federal: the parts of Medicare, enrollment periods, and penalties are overwhelmingly federal rules and are directly tested. citeturn29view1turn30view0turn11view0turn11view1  
- Texas angle (relevant because Texas exams also test state insurance regulation elsewhere): Texas has a state regulatory framework for Medicare supplement policies and consumer guidance emphasizing the **6‑month Medigap open enrollment** beginning when Part B starts. citeturn8search3turn31view3  

9) **Most likely exam traps**
- Confusing **Part C** as a “third core benefit layer” rather than an **alternative way to receive A and B** (and usually D) through a private plan. citeturn29view1turn11view1  
- Thinking Medicare covers most dental/vision/hearing or long-term custodial care; Original Medicare has notable gaps. citeturn29view0turn21search17  
- Missing that Part D is **optional** but delaying can trigger a **late enrollment penalty** absent creditable coverage. citeturn31view2turn11view0  
- Overstating Medigap’s role: Medigap generally helps with **Original Medicare (A/B) cost-sharing**; it doesn’t “upgrade” benefits to cover everything Medicare excludes and generally isn’t used with Medicare Advantage enrollment. citeturn29view1turn31view3turn4search1  

Memory aid (quick): **A = Admit**, **B = Basic medical**, **C = (private) Combination plan**, **D = Drugs**. citeturn29view1turn31view2  

### entity["organization","Medicaid","joint federal-state health coverage program"]

1) **Plain-English definition (exam-tested fact)**  
Medicaid is a **joint federal–state program** that provides health coverage to certain **low-income** individuals and specific eligibility groups (children, pregnant women, certain parents, seniors, and people with disabilities), administered by states under federal requirements and jointly funded by federal and state dollars. citeturn15view2turn15view1turn15view0  

2) **Problem the program is designed to solve (practical understanding)**  
Medicaid is designed to provide medical coverage and access to care for people who cannot reasonably afford private coverage, including long-term services and supports (LTSS) in many cases. citeturn33view0turn15view2turn15view1  

3) **Who it covers (exam-tested fact)**  
Medicaid covers **eligible low-income adults, children, pregnant women, elderly adults, and people with disabilities**, with state-specific eligibility rules. citeturn15view2turn15view1turn33view0  

4) **How eligibility generally works (exam-tested framework)**  
Eligibility is **means-tested** (income/resources and category). Federal law requires coverage of certain mandatory groups, while states can choose additional optional groups. Many eligibility pathways use Modified Adjusted Gross Income (MAGI) methods for many groups; other groups (such as age 65+ or disability-related) may follow different federal methodologies. citeturn15view1turn33view0  

5) **How benefits generally work (exam-tested at high level)**  
Medicaid benefits vary by state within federal guidelines. On the exam, the most testable “benefits concept” is that Medicaid can cover benefits **not normally covered by Medicare**, including **nursing home care and personal care services** in many circumstances. citeturn33view0turn29view1  

6) **Funding structure at a high level (testable)**  
Medicaid is jointly funded: the federal government pays a specified share of a state’s Medicaid expenditures (FMAP), and states must fund the remainder. citeturn15view0turn15view2  

7) **Coordination/interaction with private insurance (highly testable)**  
Two core coordination ideas show up repeatedly:
- **Medicaid is generally payer of last resort**: it pays after other liable payers (including private insurance) have paid. citeturn33view0turn6search8  
- For people with both Medicare and Medicaid (“dual eligible”), **Medicare pays first** for Medicare-covered services, and Medicaid pays after Medicare and other coverage. citeturn33view0turn6search3  

8) **Key federal and Texas-specific angles (exam-tested vs. background)**  
- Federal: “joint federal–state,” “means-tested,” FMAP financing, and dual-eligible payment order are directly testable. citeturn15view2turn15view0turn33view0  
- Texas-specific: administration is through Texas’s designated state agency. A CMS state plan amendment record describes Texas’s “single state agency” administration by the entity["organization","Texas Health and Human Services Commission","Austin, TX, US"] (HHSC). This is usually background for agents, but can support state-context questions around administration and eligibility operations. citeturn35search31turn15view1  

9) **Most likely exam traps**
- Choosing Medicaid when the question stem is describing **age-based eligibility (65+) without means-testing**—that’s usually Medicare, not Medicaid. citeturn29view1turn33view0  
- Forgetting that Medicaid is state-administered, so “the same in every state” is usually a wrong answer. citeturn15view2turn33view0  
- Mixing up dual-eligible payment order: **Medicare first, Medicaid last** (for Medicare-covered services). citeturn33view0  

Memory hook: **Medi-CARE = care for seniors/disabled; Medi-CAID = aid for low income**. citeturn29view1turn15view2  

### Workers’ compensation

1) **Plain-English definition (exam-tested in concept; Texas-specific details commonly tested)**  
Workers’ compensation is a state-regulated insurance program that pays benefits when employees are **injured at work** or have **work-related diseases/illnesses**, commonly including medical bills and partial wage replacement. citeturn25view0turn16view0turn16view1  

2) **Problem the program is designed to solve (practical + exam support)**  
It provides a structured system to address workplace injuries without forcing injured employees to rely solely on private health insurance or lawsuits, while also limiting employer exposure when coverage is in place. citeturn16view0turn25view2turn16view3  

3) **Who it covers (exam-tested concept)**  
Covered employees are those whose employers are “subscribers” (carry workers’ compensation coverage through a carrier or approved self-insurance), with a separate Texas structure for public entities and private employers. citeturn16view2turn25view2  

4) **How eligibility generally works (high-level; Texas exam-relevant framing)**  
Eligibility generally depends on having a compensable work-related injury/illness and the employer having coverage (subscriber) or, if not, the employee’s rights and remedies shift (non-subscriber context). citeturn16view0turn16view3turn25view1  

5) **How benefits generally work (exam-tested at high level)**  
Texas describes core benefit groupings including:  
- **Medical benefits** (reasonable and necessary medical care),  
- **Income benefits** (temporary, impairment, supplemental, lifetime),  
- **Death and burial benefits** when a worker dies due to a work-related injury/illness. citeturn16view1turn25view0  

6) **Funding structure at a high level (testable in Texas)**  
In Texas, most private employers may choose whether to carry workers’ compensation; when they do, coverage is typically funded through employer-paid premiums to insurance carriers or through qualifying self-insurance structures. citeturn25view2turn16view0turn16view2  

7) **Coordination/interaction with private insurance (testable concept)**  
Workers’ comp is designed to pay for occupational injuries/illnesses; private health insurance and disability policies frequently coordinate around occupational vs. non-occupational coverage and exclusions. Medicare also has a secondary-payer framework when another payer (including workers’ compensation) should pay first. citeturn25view0turn26view0turn32view0  

8) **Key federal and Texas-specific angles (Texas-specific is highly testable)**  
- Texas-specific “big rule”: **workers’ compensation is not required for most private employers**; employers who opt out are “non-subscribers” and have notice/reporting obligations. citeturn25view1turn16view3turn16view0  
- Texas-specific liability tradeoff: subscribers gain protection from most employee injury lawsuits; non-subscribers can lose important defenses and can be sued. citeturn16view0turn16view3turn25view2  

9) **Most likely exam traps**
- Assuming workers’ comp is mandatory for all employers in Texas (it generally isn’t for most private employers). citeturn25view1turn16view2turn16view0  
- Treating workers’ comp as “health insurance” rather than a distinct occupational injury system with its own benefit structure and employer legal protections. citeturn25view0turn16view0turn16view1  
- Missing the subscriber vs. non-subscriber consequence: non-subscribers must provide notices and can face expanded litigation exposure. citeturn16view0turn16view3  

Memory hook: **Work comp = Work cause** (if it happened “on the job,” think workers’ comp first). citeturn25view0turn16view0  

## Subtopic breakdown

### Medicare

#### Part A
**What it is:** Hospital Insurance—Part A generally helps pay for **inpatient care** in hospitals (including critical access hospitals), skilled nursing facilities, and also covers hospice care and some home health care. citeturn31view0turn29view1  

**Exam emphasis:** Identify Part A as “inpatient/hospital-related,” and recognize that Medicare measures certain use in “benefit periods,” with separate cost-sharing concepts (details/amounts are usually not state exam targets because they change annually). citeturn3search4turn21search2  

#### Part B
**What it is:** Medical Insurance—Part B covers medically necessary services and preventive services, including doctor/provider services, outpatient care, durable medical equipment (DME), and many preventive services. citeturn31view1turn29view1  

**Exam emphasis:** Part B is your “outpatient/medical” bucket and includes preventive benefits; some questions hinge on recognizing preventive services vs. medically necessary services under Part B. citeturn31view1  

#### Part C
**What it is:** Medicare Advantage—private health plans approved by Medicare that provide an alternative way to receive Medicare coverage. These “bundled” plans include **Part A and Part B** and **usually Part D**, often with network limits and potentially extra benefits beyond Original Medicare. citeturn29view1turn21search13turn11view1turn21search17  

**Exam emphasis:** Part C is not “an add-on”—it’s a **replacement path** for receiving A/B (and usually D) through a private plan. “Must cover at least what Original Medicare covers” is a common tested concept. citeturn21search17turn31view1  

#### Part D
**What it is:** Prescription drug coverage, offered by private companies approved by Medicare; optional, but delaying without creditable coverage can create a late enrollment penalty. citeturn31view2turn11view0  

**Exam emphasis:** Part D can be stand-alone with Original Medicare or included in many Medicare Advantage plans; the penalty concept is testable and the “creditable coverage” exception is a common wrong-answer trap. citeturn29view1turn11view0turn31view2  

#### Eligibility basics
Core eligibility triggers:
- Age 65 (Initial Enrollment Period framework) citeturn30view0turn30view1  
- Under 65 with disability benefits after a waiting period; ALS has an accelerated pathway; ESRD has its own eligibility and coverage start rules. citeturn30view1turn12search0turn12search7  
- Automatic vs. active enrollment depends on whether the person is already receiving Social Security benefits far enough in advance. citeturn30view1turn12search19  

#### Enrollment timing basics
High-yield enrollment periods and timing:
- **Initial Enrollment Period (IEP):** 7 months (3 months before, birthday month, 3 months after). citeturn30view0  
- **General Enrollment Period (GEP):** January 1–March 31 each year for certain late enrollees; coverage starts the month after enrollment. citeturn30view0turn11view1  
- **Medicare Advantage/Part D “Annual Open Enrollment” (Oct 15–Dec 7)** and **Medicare Advantage Open Enrollment (Jan 1–Mar 31)** appear in plan-joining rules. citeturn11view1  
- Late enrollment penalties can apply if you miss IEP and don’t qualify for an SEP. citeturn11view0turn30view0  

#### What Medicare does not cover well or fully
Original Medicare has prominent gaps that are repeatedly tested:
- **Long-term care** (custodial/nursing home “room and board” style care) citeturn29view0  
- **Most dental care** (routine services and dentures), **routine vision/eye exams for glasses**, **hearing aids**, and **routine physical exams** (as a general rule). citeturn29view0turn21search17  
- Note the exam-friendly nuance: Medicare’s dental exclusion has exceptions when dental services are integral to covered medical procedures. citeturn29view0turn3search19  

#### Relationship to Medicare supplement policies and Medicare Advantage
- **Medigap (Medicare Supplement)** is private insurance designed to help pay Original Medicare cost-sharing (copayments, coinsurance, deductibles). citeturn29view1turn21search29  
- **Medigap Open Enrollment**: a one-time 6‑month period starting when you have Part B and are 65+; during this period companies generally can’t deny coverage for health reasons. citeturn31view3turn8search3  
- Medigap generally requires **Original Medicare (A and B)**; you generally can’t use Medigap to pay Medicare Advantage cost-sharing and generally can’t buy Medigap while enrolled in Medicare Advantage unless switching back to Original Medicare. citeturn4search1turn21search7turn11view1  

### Medicaid

#### General purpose of Medicaid
Medicaid provides health coverage for eligible low-income individuals and is jointly funded by states and the federal government, administered by states under federal requirements. citeturn15view2turn15view0  

#### Basic eligibility framework
At exam level: Medicaid eligibility is **category + financial need**, with federal mandatory coverage groups and optional groups states may choose to cover. Many groups use MAGI methodology for income, while seniors/disability-related pathways may be handled differently. citeturn15view1turn33view0  

#### Federal-state structure
Medicaid is federal–state partnership:
- Federal rules define required elements and provide matching funds via FMAP. citeturn15view0turn15view2  
- States administer benefits and eligibility within those rules. citeturn15view2turn15view1  

#### How it differs from Medicare
Most testable contrasts:
- Medicare is federal health insurance primarily tied to age/disability; Medicaid is means-tested assistance tied to income/resources and category. citeturn29view1turn15view2turn33view0  
- Medicaid can cover benefits Medicare typically does not, including nursing home care and personal care services. citeturn33view0turn29view1  

#### Why it matters on a life and health insurance exam
The exam tests “public program literacy” because agents regularly encounter:
- Clients who need to understand what Medicare/Medicaid will pay before selecting supplemental private coverage, and  
- Coordination of benefits rules (Medicare primary vs. secondary; Medicaid payer of last resort). citeturn32view1turn33view0turn15view1  

### Workers’ compensation

#### General purpose
Texas frames workers’ comp as a state-regulated, **no-fault/no-blame** program for work injuries and work-related diseases/illnesses, paying medical bills and replacing some lost wages. citeturn25view0turn16view0  

#### Who is protected
Protection flows primarily to workers of **subscribing** employers (including carrier coverage and approved self-insurance structures), and Texas has required coverage for public entities described in coverage verification guidance. citeturn16view2turn25view2  

#### Covered losses at a high level
Texas benefit categories include:
- Medical benefits, income benefits (TIBs, IIBs, SIBs, LIBs), death and burial benefits. citeturn16view1  

#### Key benefit concept buckets for exam purposes
- **Medical**: reasonable and necessary medical care for the work-related injury/illness. citeturn16view1turn25view0  
- **Disability/wage replacement**: “income benefits” replace part of wage loss (with special Texas categories). citeturn16view1turn25view0  
- **Rehabilitation/return to work**: commonly discussed as part of system goals; know it exists, but the exam usually tests medical + wage replacement + legal structure more than RTW program details. citeturn24view0turn25view2  
- **Death**: benefits for beneficiaries and burial expenses. citeturn16view1turn16view0  

#### How it differs from disability income insurance and employer liability concepts
- A private disability insurance policy pays part of income when illness/injury prevents working; benefit length and definitions are policy-driven. citeturn26view0  
- Workers’ comp is tied to **work causation**, and in Texas subscribing employers receive legal protections; non-subscribers face different liability rules and notice/reporting obligations. citeturn16view0turn16view3turn25view2  

#### Texas-specific angle with high test probability
- “Not required for most private employers” and the subscriber/non-subscriber framework is a signature Texas feature. citeturn25view1turn16view0turn16view2  

## Side-by-side comparisons

### Medicare vs. Medicaid

| Feature | Medicare | Medicaid |
|---|---|---|
| Core identity | Federal health insurance (age 65+ and certain under-65 pathways) | Joint federal-state program providing coverage to certain low-income groups |
| Administration | Federal program run by CMS | State-administered under federal requirements |
| Eligibility type | Primarily age/disability/condition-driven | Means-tested (income/resources + category), varies by state |
| Benefits “headline” | Part A/B baseline, with Part C alternative and Part D drug coverage | Coverage varies by state; may cover LTSS and benefits not normally covered by Medicare |
| Dual coverage order | Medicare can pay first when both apply | Medicaid generally pays last (after Medicare and other coverage) |

Table sources: Medicare program framing and parts citeturn29view1turn12search5; Medicaid framing, state-admin, funding citeturn15view2turn15view0; dual-eligible payment order citeturn33view0.

### Medicare Part A vs. Part B vs. Part C vs. Part D

| Part | What it is | Who delivers it | What it mainly covers (exam level) | Biggest exam confusion |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | Hospital Insurance | Federal Medicare | Inpatient hospital, SNF, hospice, some home health | Mixing A with outpatient services |
| B | Medical Insurance | Federal Medicare | Doctors/providers, outpatient, DME, preventive | Thinking B is “drugs” (that’s D) |
| C | Medicare Advantage | Private Medicare-approved plans | Alternative path that bundles A + B (usually D) | Treating C as an “add-on” instead of a replacement path |
| D | Drug coverage | Private Medicare-approved plans | Prescription drugs via plan formularies/pharmacies | Confusing stand-alone Part D with drug-inclusive MA plans |

Table sources: Part A and B coverage descriptions citeturn31view0turn31view1; Part C and Part D definitions and structure citeturn29view1turn31view2turn11view1.

### Original Medicare vs. Medicare Advantage

| Dimension | Original Medicare | Medicare Advantage (Part C) |
|---|---|---|
| Structure | Part A + Part B from Medicare | Private plan alternative providing A + B benefits (usually D) |
| Provider access | Generally any provider that takes Medicare nationwide | Often network-based; plan rules apply |
| Add-ons | Can add stand-alone Part D and may buy Medigap | Often includes Part D; extra benefits may exist; Medigap generally not used with MA enrollment |
| Cost-sharing | Medicare pays part; beneficiary pays share unless supplemented | Plan-specific cost-sharing; must cover medically necessary services Original Medicare covers |

Table sources: Original vs Advantage descriptions citeturn29view1turn21search17turn11view1turn4search1.

### Medicare supplement vs. Medicaid

| Feature | Medigap (Medicare Supplement) | Medicaid |
|---|---|---|
| Type | Private insurance supplement | Public needs-based program |
| Purpose | Help pay Original Medicare cost-sharing | Provide coverage to eligible low-income groups; may assist with Medicare costs and cover additional benefits |
| Relationship to Medicare | Generally requires Original Medicare A + B | Can coexist with Medicare (dual eligible) |
| Payment order | Supplements Medicare cost-sharing depending on plan | Medicaid generally pays after Medicare and other coverage |

Table sources: Medigap basics and open enrollment citeturn31view3turn29view1turn4search1; Medicaid role and dual-eligible order citeturn33view0turn15view2.

### Workers’ compensation vs. disability income insurance

| Feature | Workers’ compensation | Disability income insurance |
|---|---|---|
| Trigger | Work-related injury/illness | Disability preventing work (policy-defined; can be illness/injury) |
| Primary benefit types | Medical + wage replacement + death/burial categories | Income replacement as % of pay; duration per policy |
| Fault concept | Texas describes as no-fault/no-blame | Not a fault system; contract benefit based on policy conditions |

## Final research memo

### Top facts to memorize from this domain
1. Social Insurance is ~6% of the Texas Life & Health exam outline and explicitly includes Medicare (A–D) and Medicaid. citeturn23view0  
2. Medicare has distinct parts: A (hospital), B (medical/outpatient), C (Medicare Advantage), D (prescription drugs). citeturn29view1turn31view0turn31view1turn31view2  
3. Part C is an alternative way to receive A and B benefits through a private Medicare-approved plan and usually includes Part D. citeturn29view1turn11view1turn21search13  
4. Part D is optional, offered through private Medicare-approved plans, and delaying enrollment can trigger late enrollment penalties (unless exceptions apply, like creditable coverage). citeturn31view2turn11view0  
5. Initial Enrollment Period is 7 months around age 65 (3 months before through 3 months after). citeturn30view0  
6. General Enrollment Period for Part B/premium-Part A runs Jan 1–Mar 31, with coverage generally starting the month after you sign up. citeturn30view0  
7. Late enrollment penalties are added to premiums, usually lasting as long as you have the coverage type; Part B and Part D penalties are common test points. citeturn11view0  
8. Medicare Trust Funds: HI funds Part A; SMI funds Parts B and D (with premiums and congressional funds). citeturn14view0  
9. Original Medicare does not cover everything; notable gaps include long-term care and most dental, plus routine vision/hearing items. citeturn29view0turn21search17  
10. Medicare Advantage plans may offer extra benefits beyond Original Medicare (vision/hearing/dental are commonly cited examples). citeturn29view0turn21search13turn21search17  
11. Medicaid is a joint federal and state program, administered by states under federal requirements and jointly funded. citeturn15view2turn15view0  
12. Medicaid eligibility is generally means-tested and varies by state; federal law requires certain mandatory groups. citeturn15view1turn15view2  
13. Medicaid can offer benefits not normally covered by Medicare (nursing home care and personal care services are commonly cited examples). citeturn33view0turn29view1  
14. If a person has both Medicare and full Medicaid, they are “dually eligible.” citeturn33view0turn6search3  
15. For dual eligibles, Medicare pays first for Medicare-covered services; Medicaid pays last after Medicare and other coverage. citeturn33view0  
16. Medicare coordination of benefits uses payer order (primary vs secondary). citeturn32view1turn32view0  
17. Texas workers’ comp is described as state-regulated, no-fault/no-blame for workplace injuries/illnesses and pays medical bills and replaces some lost wages. citeturn25view0  
18. In Texas, workers’ comp is not required for most private employers. citeturn25view1turn16view2turn16view0  
19. Texas workers’ comp benefits include income benefits (TIBs/IIBs/SIBs/LIBs), medical benefits, and death/burial benefits. citeturn16view1  
20. Texas non-subscribers must provide notices (posting and written notice to new employees) and file certain notices/reporting as described by TDI/DWC guidance. citeturn16view3turn16view0
